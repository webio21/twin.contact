Abstract

Abstract

As generative AI systems move from short exchanges toward sustained collaboration and task execution, personalization requires more than retaining conversation history or a static preference profile. A system must form a revisable account of a person from heterogeneous information distributed across services, authored by different people, and subject to change over time. It must also distinguish what is known, what is inferred, the context in which a statement applies, and the purposes for which that information may be used.

This paper proposes the Contact Twin Personal Intelligence Framework as a user-governed conceptual model and reference architecture. It organizes personal intelligence through four related semantic domains: Knowledge & Experience, Thinking & Judgment, Values & Goals, and Personality & Preferences. It distinguishes one global Primary Twin from purpose-scoped Scenario Twins. It further introduces Fusion, a task-scoped composition of separately authorized intelligence from multiple Twins, and Purpose-Bound Intelligence Projection (PBIP), a proposed contract for determining what intelligence may be delivered to a Fusion or another downstream application according to purpose, participant, scope, time, and valid authorization.

The framework adopts personal-intelligence mirroring as a central design principle: the ongoing construction and maintenance of an external, revisable, evidence-grounded representation of selected aspects of a person, bounded by context and user governance.

The contribution is an integrated set of architectural design decisions concerning semantic organization, global-to-scenario specialization, non-destructive multi-Twin composition, and governed delivery. The four domains are not presented as a definitive psychological taxonomy, and a Twin is not a replication of consciousness or a complete human identity. This is a conceptual and architectural proposal, not an empirical evaluation. Its propositions require implementation studies, security testing, longitudinal user research, and controlled comparisons.

Keywords: Personal intelligence; personal-intelligence mirroring; personal memory; user modeling; Primary Twin; Scenario Twin; Twin Fusion; purpose-bound intelligence projection; contextual governance; human–AI interaction; AI agents.
Scope and status. This paper proposes a conceptual model and reference architecture. It is not a product-effectiveness study, a systematic literature review, a standardized technical specification, or a validated security protocol. PBIP is a proposed architectural and operational contract, not an established standard.

Central proposition

Personal intelligence should be able to develop and be corrected over time, specialize by context, be composed for a task, and be delivered only within the user's authorization.

The objective is not to accumulate the largest possible quantity of personal data. It is to maintain an account of a person whose sources, temporal validity, and applicable scope can be inspected, corrected, and governed by that person.

The framework begins with a design distinction:

Personal Data ≠ Personal Memory ≠ Personal Intelligence

This is a conceptual distinction for system design, not a set of mutually exclusive legal categories. Personal Data refers to data supplied, generated, or authorized for processing. Personal Memory refers to retained and traceable items organized from such data. Personal Intelligence refers to an application-oriented understanding formed through relationships among memories, evidence, time, and context. Personal memories and intelligence may themselves constitute personal data and remain subject to applicable legal and governance requirements.

Introduction: From cross-tool memory to governed personal intelligence

As AI moves from one-off question answering toward ongoing collaboration, the central requirement is not simply a larger context window. It is a continuing, but revisable, understanding of a person's knowledge, experience, judgment, goals, and preferences.

Information about a person is distributed across documents, email, messages, calendars, projects, workflows, and multiple AI services. A single platform usually sees only part of this context. Conversely, indiscriminately aggregating all available data can produce excessive collection, mistaken attribution, stale information, and leakage between contexts. Personalization is therefore both a modeling problem and a governance problem. A system must identify which information may describe the person, when and under what circumstances it applies, and who may use it for which purpose.

Research on autobiographical memory, user modeling, personal informatics, and recent LLM-agent memory addresses memory construction, user representations, reflection, and information retention during extended interaction [1–8]. A long-term architecture for personal intelligence must additionally define the relationship between global and scenario-specific knowledge, the composition of intelligence from independent Twins, and the authorization boundary governing delivery to downstream applications.

Research questions

  1. How can heterogeneous and time-varying personal information be organized into a model with traceable sources, temporal validity, explicit scope, and correction mechanisms?
  2. How can a stable and understandable semantic structure represent knowledge and experience, thinking and judgment, values and goals, and personality and preferences?
  3. How can a system preserve global coherence while creating scenario-specific Twins that do not contaminate one another?
  4. How can independent Twins contribute authorized intelligence to a shared task without merging their permanent identities or creating a duplicate authoritative memory store?
  5. How can personal intelligence be delivered to a Fusion or another downstream application through inspectable, limited, and revocable authorization?
  6. How should the architecture's effects on task utility, user agency, and privacy risk be evaluated together?

Architectural contributions

  1. A distinction among personal data, personal memory, and personal intelligence for system modeling.
  2. A semantic model with four related domains and two cross-cutting contextual layers.
  3. A structure in which one Primary Twin provides the global foundation and Scenario Twins provide scoped specialization.
  4. Fusion as an authorized, task-scoped composition of multiple Twins rather than a new permanent owner of merged intelligence.
  5. PBIP as a proposed purpose-bound contract for delivering intelligence to a Fusion or another downstream application.
  6. Governance requirements for provenance, evidence, time, scope, consent, revocation, downstream responsibility, and future evaluation.

Method and scope

This paper follows a conceptual architecture approach. It uses selected adjacent research to establish the problem context, then derives design requirements from longitudinal personal intelligence, scenario isolation, multi-party collaboration, and cross-application delivery. It proposes definitions, architectural relationships, and testable propositions. It is not a systematic literature review and does not report a completed product implementation or user experiment. The proposal should be assessed through conceptual clarity, operational feasibility, governance completeness, and subsequent empirical evidence.

Related work and theoretical positioning

This is a thematic positioning of selected work, not a systematic review. Its purpose is to identify problems already addressed by neighboring research, clarify their scope, and locate the architectural decisions proposed here.

Autobiographical memory and personal knowledge

Conway and Pleydell-Pearce's Self-Memory System describes autobiographical memory as a construction shaped by the interaction between personal knowledge and current goals, rather than as a simple list of stored events [1]. This perspective highlights the relationship between experience, goals, and self-understanding. The present paper does not propose a psychological theory of memory. It asks how personal information with traceable provenance can be represented in an inspectable, revisable, and governed application model.

User modeling and personalization

User-modeling research has long examined reusable representations of individuals and the separation of user-model services from single applications [2, 3]. This work provides an important foundation for cross-application personal models. Contact Twin extends the design question to long-term experience, judgment, values, and goals, while treating source, time, context, and authorization as essential model attributes rather than optional settings.

Personal informatics and reflection

Personal-informatics research studies how people collect, integrate, reflect on, and act upon personal information, and identifies barriers across these stages [4]. It helps explain how individuals may develop self-knowledge from their own data. The present framework focuses more directly on the architecture of a persistent intelligence service: how information enters and changes a model, how it applies across contexts, and how other applications may use it.

Memory, reflection, and long-term interaction in LLM agents

Generative Agents combine experience records, reflection, and retrieval to study how simulated characters produce later behavior [5]. Reflexion uses language-based feedback to improve an agent's subsequent task performance [6]. MemGPT explores hierarchical context management for extended interaction and document handling [7]. A more recent survey organizes mechanisms for memory in LLM-based agents, including sources, representations, operations, and evaluation [8]. These works primarily study agent performance, simulated behavior, or context management. Contact Twin instead models intelligence about real people and must therefore address data-subject rights, mistaken attribution, cross-context isolation, and authorization transfer. An agent's reflection must not automatically be treated as a person's stable belief or personality.

Multi-agent collaboration and multi-Twin composition

Multi-agent systems research examines how relatively independent agents coordinate, divide work, and solve problems together [9]. Twin Fusion is related, but addresses an additional question: before collaboration begins, which parts of each participant's personal intelligence may be used for the shared task? Fusion therefore needs to preserve each participant's authorization and provenance boundaries throughout composition. Membership in the same team cannot be treated as permission to share all personal information.

Contextual privacy, access control, and human–AI interaction

Contextual Integrity evaluates information flows in relation to the norms of a particular context [10]. Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC) provides established mechanisms for deciding authorization from attributes associated with subjects, resources, operations, and environmental conditions [11]. Human–AI interaction guidelines emphasize that systems should communicate capabilities and limitations, help users understand errors, and preserve appropriate user control [12].

On 18 December 2025, the Stanford Digital Economy Lab published the working paper Robust AI Personalization Controls: The Human Context Protocol. It proposes HCP as a user-centric, portable layer for personal preferences across AI systems and discusses interoperability, scoped access, and revocation [13]. On 16 March 2026, the Lab published The Future of Personal AI, a research report that further addresses portable and persistent personal memory and selective sharing by role, consent, and purpose [14]. These works substantially overlap with this paper's problem space of cross-tool personal context, user governance, and controlled sharing; the HCP working paper also predates the version date of this paper. Accordingly, this paper does not claim to originate portable personal memory, cross-application context exchange, scoped access, or purpose-selective sharing. The proposed integration—four semantic domains, Primary/Scenario specialization, non-destructive Fusion among independent Twins, and the PBIP task-delivery contract—should be treated as architectural decisions requiring direct comparison with HCP at the levels of functionality, data model, and governance. Reference [13] is a working paper record and [14] an institutional research report; neither is represented here as a peer-reviewed journal article.

Conceptual model: Defining personal intelligence

Personal Intelligence is defined here as a continuously revisable model of a person that maintains relationships to sources and evidence, temporal validity, contextual scope, and mechanisms for user governance. It organizes knowledge and experience, thinking and judgment, values and goals, and personality and preferences, then supplies bounded context for a particular task.

Personal-intelligence mirroring as a design principle

In this paper, personal-intelligence mirroring refers to the ongoing construction and maintenance of an external representation of selected aspects of a person. The representation is grounded in traceable, appropriately authorized evidence and covers the person's knowledge and experience, thinking and judgment, values and goals, and personality and preferences. It retains provenance, temporal validity, contextual scope, and uncertainty, and remains open to the person's inspection, correction, restriction, and withdrawal of authorization.

A Twin is a governed model about a person. Its accuracy is bounded by available evidence, data quality, and the context in which information was obtained; it cannot represent the person's entire experience, consciousness, or identity. Personal-intelligence mirroring is the design principle for constructing and maintaining Personal Intelligence, while the four semantic domains provide its organizing structure.

Classification boundary. The four domains are architectural categories intended to support usability, engineering consistency, and research operationalization. They are not a psychometrically validated theory of personality, a complete cognitive-science taxonomy, or a clinical instrument.

Knowledge & Experience

What a person knows and has experienced
WHAT

Core question: What knowledge, capabilities, and experiences can be attributed to the person?

This domain may include education and professional background, knowledge, skills, areas of expertise, work and life experience, project outcomes, successes, failures, and lessons formed through practice. The system must distinguish knowledge appearing in a document from knowledge or capability that the user has personally acquired or confirmed.

Thinking & Judgment

How a person reasons and decides
HOW

Core question: How does the person understand problems, reason, evaluate, and make judgments?

This domain may include analytical approaches, reasoning strategies, problem solving, decision frameworks, risk evaluation, trade-off criteria, and reflection. The relevant record is not only the conclusion but also its reasons, evidence, assumptions, and uncertainty. One decision does not establish a stable thinking pattern; repeated observations should initially remain candidate interpretations for review.

Values & Goals

What a person values and seeks
WHY

Core question: What does the person consider important, believe, and seek to achieve?

This domain may include beliefs, principles, motivations, priorities, life and career goals, business objectives, and long-term aspirations. Values and goals should not be inferred solely from purchases, clicks, or work behavior. Explicit statements or confirmation by the user should be the primary basis. Goals also require temporal and status information because they may be completed, paused, changed, or become obsolete.

Personality & Preferences

Relatively stable traits and contextual preferences
STYLE

Core question: Which relatively stable characteristics and interaction preferences are relevant?

This domain may include communication style, aesthetic and choice preferences, work habits, collaboration style, and possible behavioral tendencies. Relatively stable characteristics must be distinguished from context-dependent preferences. A single mood or behavior should not become a permanent label. Inferences should remain uncertain, inspectable, and deletable; the system should not perform unconsented psychological labeling or clinical diagnosis.

The four domains are multi-label semantic indices

A single event may concern professional experience, judgment, personal values, and interaction preferences at the same time. The domains should therefore support multiple labels and relationships rather than forcing every memory into one exclusive folder.

Two cross-cutting contextual layers

Identity & Relationships

Identity, roles, relationships, family, colleagues, clients, partners, and organizations. This layer helps identify who is involved, who authored a statement, and what role the user occupies in a relationship.

State & Context

Time, location, current projects, life stages, environment, deadlines, and temporary constraints. This layer records when and under what circumstances an item applies.

A conceptual representation

For a person u at time t, personal intelligence can be represented as a set of memory items with semantic labels and governance metadata:

PIu(t) = { Mu(t), D, IR, SC, G }

M: the set of memory items; D: the four semantic domains, which may be multi-labeled; IR: Identity & Relationships; SC: State & Context; G: governance metadata such as provenance, evidence, time, scope, consent, and lifecycle.

This representation does not require every domain to be populated. Information may be sparse, uncertain, contradictory, or time-varying. The model should preserve those conditions rather than compressing uncertain material into a single persona description.

Primary, Scenario, and Fusion: Global, contextual, and multi-party structure

The structure of personal intelligence must address global coherence for one person, contextual differences, and collaboration among independent Twins. These are separate responsibilities and should not be treated as one undifferentiated merging operation.

Figure 1 · Three complementary structural roles

Figure 1. A and B represent independently governed participant Twins. Each data subject receives a separate PBIP authorization decision, and Fusion composes only the portions each participant permits. The pattern can extend to more than two participants.

Primary Twin: one global foundation for each person

Within this framework, each person has one Primary Twin as the global personal-intelligence model across contexts. It need not store every application's raw data and should not collapse all contexts into one undifferentiated summary. It maintains personal-intelligence items with provenance, time, and scope, then supplies relevant subsets according to an authorized task.

Scenario Twin: specialization within a defined context

A Scenario Twin may correspond to a brand, profession, specialist domain, project, client relationship, or continuing work context. It combines relevant and authorized projections from the Primary Twin with scenario-specific knowledge, rules, goals, state, and history. It is not another complete person and should not duplicate the entire Primary Twin.

ScenarioTwinu,s(t) = View(PrimaryTwinu, AuthorizedScopes) ⊕ ScenarioDatas

Here, u denotes the person, s the scenario, and t the time of the view. The operator ⊕ denotes a policy-governed contextual composition, not unconditional data merging. New observations made within a scenario remain local by default; they become global personal intelligence only after user confirmation. For authoritative memory, the preferred implementation is traceable references and dynamic authorized views rather than another copy requiring continuous synchronization. If runtime materialization is necessary, it is derived data associated with a source version and expiry, not a new authoritative record.

Fusion: authorized composition of multiple Twins for a task

Fusion is a task-scoped intelligence view formed from two or more independent Twins for a defined purpose. Participants may be different people's Twins or different Scenario Twins. Only intelligence that each participant separately authorizes and that is relevant to the shared task may be included. Fusion manages which Twins participate, under what scopes, and for which task; it does not merge identities or combine all memories into a new permanent Twin.

A Fusion may be created for a single task or saved as a reusable composition configuration. The configuration may record participating Twins, authorized scopes, task purpose, roles, policy version, expiry, and participant status. It should not store a duplicate authoritative copy of all fused intelligence. A task may require a short-lived context or cache, but such material is derived data subject to access, expiry, and deletion rules, not a separate permanent intelligence layer.

When a participant leaves or revokes authorization, the system should stop future delivery and recompute the Fusion view; controlled caches and derived data should be invalidated where required. The system cannot promise to retrieve information already read by a participant, processed by a model, or embedded in an existing output. Downstream retention, deletion, and output-reuse rules must therefore be defined in advance.

Organizational contexts

An organization may maintain its own organizational model for goals, processes, policies, knowledge, and decision history. Members' Primary Twins remain under individual control. The organization may receive only information or projections that members explicitly authorize for defined purposes and scopes. Employment, team membership, or participation in a Fusion does not imply permission to combine private Twin data. Ownership, retention responsibilities, and post-exit handling must remain distinct for organizational models and personal Twins.

Learning architecture: From explicit statements to authorized experience

Personal intelligence cannot be responsibly constructed from a single source type. Interviews can reveal reasons and tacit knowledge; documents provide traceable statements and work products; authorized connections may provide naturally occurring work and interaction context; ongoing feedback helps determine whether the model remains current. Read access, learning permission, retention permission, and downstream sharing permission should be governed separately.

Interview

Use concrete cases, decision reviews, and follow-up questions to understand how a person reasons, why a choice was made, and what mattered. Distinguish exploratory thoughts, temporary states, and confirmed long-term positions.

Knowledge import

Extract traceable information from résumés, work samples, reports, notes, and documents. The presence of knowledge in a file does not establish that the user personally knows or endorses it.

Connected sources

Retrieve only the accounts, folders, documents, conversations, calendars, projects, or time ranges selected by the user. Access does not automatically imply permission to learn from data or transfer it to another application.

Experience feedback

Use corrections, confirmations, task reviews, and explicit feedback to create candidate updates. A single action may reflect situational constraints; high-impact inferences about values, goals, or personality should remain tentative and require confirmation.

Three authorization dimensions: source, scope, and purpose

  1. Source: select the data source or service.
  2. Scope: select the account, folder, document, conversation, contact, project, or time range.
  3. Purpose: separately specify whether the data may be read, used for Twin learning, retained, applied to a scenario, or delivered for a named downstream purpose.

Email, chat, and meeting content must retain author and participant attribution. A user's access to a source does not mean that all third-party information within it may be permanently modeled or shared.

Interviews should be grounded in cases and context

Rather than asking only, “What is your decision-making style?”, the system should invite the person to review a concrete decision: what alternatives were available, which factors mattered, what was rejected, what happened, and whether the person would make the same choice today. This approach can preserve reasoning context, but one case must not be generalized into a permanent trait.

Provenance and epistemic status are separate

Provenance identifies where information came from, such as Interview, Drive, Mail, or Run. Epistemic status identifies how the system knows it, such as User-stated, Observed, Externally verified, or Inferred. These are independent dimensions. A memory may originate in an interview and be a user's statement; another may originate in a public document and be independently verified. Neither should be compressed into a single generic “trust” label.

The personal-intelligence lifecycle

The framework proposes a traceable cycle linking acquisition, semantic interpretation, evidence review, memory integration, contextual specialization, purpose-bound delivery, and subsequent correction. This is a system-design proposal, not a claim that all cognition follows one universal sequence.

Capture and interpret

The system records source references and identifies claims, events, preferences, goals, people, and scenarios. It must distinguish the speaker, author, and subject described in a conversation or document. Interpretations initially become candidate memories rather than established personal characteristics.

Validate and integrate

Important memories retain evidence, epistemic status, confidence, uncertainty, and possible counter-evidence. Integration may involve deduplication, entity linking, conflict handling, versioning, and temporal validity. New information may supersede older information without erasing historical context that remains explanatory. Sensitive or deletion-requested information must be handled under the applicable policy.

Specialize and compose

The Primary Twin provides the global foundation. A Scenario Twin receives only relevant authorized information and adds scenario data. When a task requires multiple Twins, Fusion obtains separately authorized projections through PBIP and forms a task-scoped view. Neither operation changes the authoritative ownership of the source Twins.

Serve, reflect, and evolve

Downstream applications receive only the projection necessary for the task. Corrections from Answer, outcomes from Run, and explicit user feedback may generate candidate updates. Whether an update belongs in the Primary Twin or should remain within a Scenario must depend on source, time, scope, and user confirmation. A task output should not automatically become part of a person's identity or beliefs.

Reference architecture and the PBIP contract

The reference architecture separates source connections, evidence and memory, Twin structure, policy decisions, delivery, and audit. Downstream applications do not directly read the complete personal data store. Delivery of personal intelligence must pass through purpose and authorization checks. In a multi-party Fusion, PBIP is evaluated independently for each data subject; Fusion receives only the projections each participant separately authorizes.

PBIP: Purpose-Bound Intelligence Projection

PBIP is proposed as an architectural delivery model and operational contract for personal intelligence. It represents the interface between a personal-intelligence model and a downstream application as a policy-governed projection, rather than allowing the application to read the complete Twin. Each projection should identify the requesting application, task purpose, data subject, target scenario, permitted operations, data scope, validity period, and consent state.

PBIP(u, a, q, t) = SelectMin { m ∈ PIu(t) | R(m, a, q, t) = allow }

Here, u is the subject of the personal intelligence; a is the requesting downstream application; q is the task request, including its purpose and required scope; t is the decision time; m is an individual memory item; and R is the policy-decision function. allow denotes a successful policy decision. PIu(t) is the subject's personal-intelligence set at time t. SelectMin selects the least-disclosing set among items that satisfy task necessity and authorization. This is an architectural expression, not a proven optimization algorithm; an implementation must define measurable task sufficiency, disclosure cost, and policy-conflict handling.

The value claim of PBIP is not that it invents purpose limitation, data minimization, or attribute-based access control. Its proposed contribution is to apply related principles at the system boundary where personal intelligence is composed and delivered as semantic information. PBIP is not a cryptographic protocol, a universal access-control standard, or a security implementation already shown to be safe. Its incremental value must be evaluated against existing policy and access-control models.

Technical limits of revocation

Revocation can stop future reads and projection requests and can require downstream systems to invalidate or delete controlled caches, indexes, and derived data according to agreed rules. It cannot guarantee retrieval of information already read by a person, processed by a model, or included in an existing output. A system must not describe revocation as physical recall of information already disclosed. Projections should have expiry, recipient obligations, retention rules, and auditable revocation events. Where legal or security audit records cannot be deleted, only the minimum necessary metadata should be retained where possible.

Model independence and human understandability

The data structure, provenance, and governance policies for personal intelligence should remain independent of any single model provider. A model may interpret a projection, but it does not thereby become the owner of the underlying personal intelligence. Users should be able to inspect and correct important memories in understandable language, while the system preserves structured metadata for machine processing.

Application boundaries: Separate understanding, analysis, and execution

This section uses Contact applications as an architectural example, not as a product specification or commercial claim. Different applications may use the same Twin foundation, but their tasks, tool permissions, and responsibilities should remain distinct.

Twin · Maintain understanding

Build, organize, inspect, and correct personal intelligence. Present candidate interpretations with their provenance and uncertainty. Maintaining personal intelligence does not itself grant authority to take external actions.

Answer · Analyze and verify

Handle requests requiring analysis, comparison, evidence checks, or synthesis. Answer may use an authorized Twin projection, but does not thereby receive persistent-task or external-execution authority.

Run · Delegate and execute

Handle authorized task plans, tool calls, scheduling, monitoring, and delivery. Task outcomes may become candidate learning signals for the Twin, but should not silently rewrite global identity or values.

Context and Engine · Shared infrastructure

Context manages memory, relationships, and task context. Engine provides model routing, reasoning, verification, and synthesis. These are shared infrastructure services, not additional conceptual layers users must manage.

Governance and trust: Permissions, provenance, and responsibility

The more complete a personal-intelligence model becomes, the greater the risks of mistaken attribution, stale information, third-party data misuse, and cross-context disclosure. Governance must therefore begin when information enters the model and continue through specialization, Fusion, and downstream delivery. It cannot be reduced to a user setting applied after the fact.

Core governance principles

  1. Separate permissions. Connecting a source does not imply permission to learn from it. Reading, retention, inference, sharing, and downstream use should be authorized according to distinct purposes.
  2. Purpose limitation and data minimization. Each delivery contains only the authorized intelligence items necessary for the specified task.
  3. Transparent provenance and epistemic status. Users can distinguish self-reports, observations, external verification, and inference. Model speculation must not be presented as a fact confirmed by the person.
  4. Inspection and correction. Users can review important interpretations, sources, and epistemic status and request correction, downgrade, exclusion, or deletion.
  5. Revocation and temporal validity. Revocation should prevent future access and trigger downstream invalidation procedures. Expired information must not remain valid by default.
  6. Cross-context and multi-party isolation. Information about separate clients, brands, projects, and participants must not be automatically shared merely because it belongs to one person or appears in one Fusion.

Multi-party authorization in Fusion

The creator of a Fusion cannot authorize another participant's private intelligence on that participant's behalf. Each Twin owner must appropriately authorize the scope, task purpose, receiving role, and validity period of the information contributed. Where a source contains third-party data, the Twin owner's consent may not be sufficient to authorize all information about those third parties. Depending on the data and applicable law, the system may need to exclude, mask, summarize, or seek additional authorization for that content.

Organizational models and personal Twins

An organization may own an organizational intelligence model, but it should not treat members' Primary Twins as organizational assets. Information provided by an individual to an organization should have a defined purpose and scope. Departure from employment, exit from a project, or revocation should trigger enforceable access termination, data handling, and audit processes.

PBIP in relation to existing governance principles

PBIP is related to purpose limitation, data minimization, Contextual Integrity, and ABAC [10, 11]. This paper does not describe it as a new access-control standard that replaces those methods. Its research value depends on whether task purpose, Twin identity, scenario scope, participant authorization, expiry, and deliverable semantic items can be combined into an inspectable contract for personal-intelligence delivery.

Evaluation framework: Measure utility and risk together

Personal intelligence should not be evaluated solely by memory volume, retrieval hit rate, conversation count, or time spent in a product. Evaluation must also ask whether the model understands the person accurately, whether information is traceable, whether scenarios remain isolated, whether permissions are enforced, and whether downstream task benefits justify the added risk.

Suggested empirical design

  1. Benchmark dataset. With informed consent and appropriate de-identification, create evaluation data labeled for source, time, person, and scope.
  2. Comparison conditions. Compare no personal context, a static personal summary, conventional long-term memory, and a condition with Primary/Scenario structure, provenance governance, and PBIP.
  3. Scenario experiments. Use similar tasks with different brands, clients, or roles to measure isolation and mistaken cross-context retrieval.
  4. Fusion experiments. Test authorized composition of two or more Twins, participant exit, cache invalidation after revocation, and preservation of multi-party provenance.
  5. Longitudinal pilot. Conduct follow-up over weeks or months. Sample size and duration should be determined by statistical power, risk, and ethics review rather than assuming a fixed study is adequate.
  6. Integrated risk assessment. An increase in task utility must not by itself establish success if mistaken attribution, unauthorized disclosure, or loss of user control also increases.

Testable research propositions

The following propositions are hypotheses for empirical testing, not findings established by this paper.

H1 · Contextual utility

Provenance-aware intelligence improves relevance

Compared with no personal context or a static summary, personal intelligence with source and time information is expected to improve relevance for selected tasks.

H2 · Scenario specialization

Specialization balances global coherence and isolation

A Primary/Scenario structure is expected to preserve cross-context coherence while reducing context mixing compared with a single global summary.

H3 · Provenance

Source and epistemic labels reduce mistaken attribution

Recording source, author, evidence, and epistemic status is expected to reduce the rate at which others' views or model inferences are misrepresented as user statements.

H4 · PBIP

Purpose-bound projection reduces over-disclosure

Joint decisions based on task, scope, time, and authorization are expected to disclose less irrelevant information than sharing an entire personal profile.

H5 · User agency

Inspection and correction improve perceived control

Helping users understand sources, inference status, and authorized purposes is expected to improve correction success and comprehension of system control.

H6 · Fusion integrity

Non-destructive Fusion preserves participant boundaries

Saving composition settings instead of permanent merged memory is expected to reduce ownership and synchronization problems when participants leave or revoke access.

H7 · Longitudinal learning

Revisable updates outperform static personas

A model with temporal validity, provenance, and user correction is expected to better reflect changes in goals, roles, and preferences.

H8 · Downstream role separation

Separating understanding, analysis, and execution reduces intent confusion

With authorization context shared appropriately, separating personal-intelligence maintenance, analytical verification, and task execution is expected to reduce routing errors and unintended actions.

Research agenda

This paper does not propose a ten-year product roadmap. Future work should be driven by testable questions and known risks, with scope expanded only as evidence warrants.

Validity of the personal-intelligence representation

Research should test whether the four semantic domains are sufficiently stable and understandable to users, and whether subcategories or representations need adjustment across cultures, professions, and life contexts. It should also evaluate the quality of personal-intelligence mirroring through representational fidelity, evidence traceability, temporal accuracy, contextual appropriateness, uncertainty calibration, and responsiveness to user correction. Each dimension requires an operational definition and empirical testing.

Longitudinal learning and memory revision

Research should compare the evidential quality of interviews, documents, observed activity, and user feedback. It should examine how to prevent over-inference, memory drift, duplication, and erroneous updates, and when user confirmation is necessary for different memory types.

Scenario isolation and multi-Twin Fusion

Scenario boundaries, conflict handling, and authorization propagation require formalization. Dynamic views, short-lived caches, and controlled materialization should be compared. Fusion particularly requires study of participant exit, revocation, deletion, and the lifecycle of derived outputs.

PBIP interoperability with existing access control

PBIP should be mapped concretely to ABAC, purpose limitation, data minimization, policy-enforcement points, and audit frameworks. The reliability, latency, understandability, and verifiability of policy decisions should be measured.

User agency and social impact

Research should examine whether people understand how a Twin represents them, can correct mistaken inferences, and can govern third-party data. It should also investigate risks to autonomy, bias, and concentration of power that may result from long-term dependence on personal-intelligence systems.

Limitations, risks, and open questions

  1. A conceptual model is not a scientific taxonomy. The four domains are architectural categories and have not been validated through psychometric or cross-cultural studies.
  2. Personal intelligence cannot be fully digitized. Subjective experience, bodily states, unconscious processes, and social circumstances cannot be completely represented by finite data.
  3. Inference can be biased. Behavior may reflect tool defaults, external constraints, or another person's requirements rather than a stable user preference.
  4. Evidence quality varies. Documents, narratives, observations, and model inferences have different reliability. A single confidence score cannot resolve every form of uncertainty.
  5. Cross-context risk remains. A shared personal model may cause client, brand, family, or professional information to be retrieved in the wrong context. Isolation requires continuous testing.
  6. Fusion revocation has limits. A system can stop future access and manage controlled copies, but cannot guarantee recall of information already read, processed, or embedded in existing outputs.
  7. PBIP's novelty is not established. Its constituent principles overlap with purpose limitation, minimization, and ABAC. Further comparison and implementation tests are needed to establish whether it provides material incremental value.
  8. Legal and ethical applicability varies. Personal intelligence, third-party data, and organizational use may engage different data-protection and employment rules across jurisdictions. This paper is not legal advice.
  9. No empirical results are reported. All propositions require implementation, user research, security testing, and reproducible evaluation.

Conclusion: A maintainable, specialized, composable, and governed model

The long-term value of personalization lies not simply in remembering more, but in distinguishing sources, time, and context correctly while respecting each data subject's control over their information.

This paper has proposed the Contact Twin Personal Intelligence Framework: a user-governed model that records provenance, evidence, temporal validity, and scope. Four semantic domains provide an organizing structure. The Primary Twin maintains one person's global model, while Scenario Twins specialize relevant intelligence within defined contexts.

Fusion addresses a different problem. It composes separately authorized intelligence from multiple Twins for a specified task without merging permanent identities or creating an authoritative merged-memory layer. A reusable Fusion should retain composition settings rather than duplicate complete personal-intelligence stores. PBIP determines what intelligence may be delivered to a Fusion or another downstream application and places purpose, subject, scope, time, and authorization within the delivery contract.

These architectural decisions do not replace existing work on memory, user modeling, privacy, or access control. They form a set of design propositions that must be examined through literature comparison, engineering implementation, governance testing, and longitudinal research. The framework's feasibility ultimately depends on whether it can improve personalization while maintaining provenance quality, user agency, multi-party boundaries, and enforceable limits on future access.
